Assessment 001

Standard Deadline: Monday, 4 January 2027, 3:00 PM

1. Tutor with responsibility for this assignment 

Alex Kyalo [akyalo1@glos.ac.uk] is your first point of contact for this element of assessment.  

2. The requirements for this assignment 

Modern applications are assembled from source code, frameworks, third-party dependencies, APIs, authentication services, build systems and deployment configurations. Security failures can therefore originate from insecure code, inappropriate design decisions, vulnerable dependencies, configuration errors or weaknesses in the software build and delivery process. 

Secure software development requires more than fixing vulnerabilities after they have been discovered. Developers must identify threats, implement appropriate controls, use security testing tools, evaluate the limitations of those tools and produce evidence that mitigations remain effective throughout the software development lifecycle. 

In this assessment, you will create, review and improve a collection of controlled laboratory examples and produce a portfolio that addresses vulnerabilities (shown under vulnerability classes) by: 

  • Explaining and contextualising the vulnerability by explain the underlying security theory and identifying the relevant weakness using an appropriate classification, such as CWE or OWASP; 

  • Demonstrating how a vulnerability can be exploited with code examples 

  • Demonstrating how the code was tested to identify the vulnerability using appropriate techniques and tools, such as SAST, DAST, SCA, fuzzing, runtime memory-safety testing, automated unit, integration or regression testing and more. 

  • Demonstrating how to mitigate and what security measures were put into place with justification 

  • Testing again to make sure the code is resilient to the chosen attack 

Your portfolio must include one case study from each of the following vulnerability classes: 

  1. Web application or identity security: examples include injection, cross-site scripting, broken access control, insecure session management, inappropriate OAuth 2.0 implementation or ineffective anti-automation controls. 

  1. Unsafe Java deserialization or software/data integrity 

  1. Cryptographic or secrets-management failure 

  1. Memory-safety failure: This may include a buffer overflow, use-after-free, integer overflow or another appropriate memory corruption vulnerability. 

  1. Software supply-chain or build-integrity failure: examples include a vulnerable dependency, dependency confusion, insecure package selection, an exposed secret, an unverified build artefact or an insecure CI/CD configuration. 

Your portfolio should also include general discussion about: 

  • The importance of Security Development Life Cycle 

  • Product risk assessment and risk analysis 

 
Word Count
3000 words (+/- 10%) excluding title pages, references and appendix sections 

Arrangements for working

You should work alone on this assessment. 

Referencing 

Careful referencing of sources is vital when making use of the work of others.  You are expected to follow the Harvard style referencing conventions required by the Course.  These conventions apply to information taken from internet sources, as well as books, journals and lectures.  These are some of the points you should check before submitting your work:

  • are all direct quotations, from both primary and secondary sources, suitably acknowledged (placed in quotation marks or indented)?  
  • have you provided full details of the source of the quotation, according to the referencing convention used in the Course?  
  • have you acknowledged the source of ideas not your own, even if you are not quoting directly from the source? 
  • have you avoided close paraphrase from sources? (Check that you are not presenting the words or phrasing of others as if they are your own.)  
  • if you have worked closely with others in preparing for this assessment, is the material you are presenting sufficiently your own?  
  • have you listed the full publication details of your sources, formatted according to the referencing system required by the Course and typically located at the end of a submission?
  • for peer-reviewed journal articles, have you included the doi address (direct weblink to the paper) within that list? 

3. Use of Artificial Intelligence 

This assignment is AI Category 2 (Minimal) 

For this assignment, you can, for example:

  • Brainstorming ideas and questions; drafting outlines/structures. 

  • Language help: grammar/style suggestions; paraphrasing your own sentences to improve clarity without changing your argument. 

  • Time management aids (checklists, study plans). 

However, you cannot, use AI to, for example: 

  • Writing assessment content for you (sections, essays, reports, code, artwork) unless the task explicitly allows it. 

  • Generating references/citations you have not checked (AI can invent sources). 

  • Uploading/pasting confidential material (unpublished data, client information, personal data, complete assignments). 

  • Bypassing the reading, analysis, calculations or design you are being assessed on. 

  • Any use that breaches the assessment brief, copyright or Academic Integrity rules 

For further information, see your school’s BCSS Statement on Acknowledging AI in Student Work, and the university’s Student Guidance for using Generative AI in learning

YOU MUST provide an AI disclosure statement, which should be included in after the conclusion before the reference list.  

This includes disclosure where AI has not been used. 

Declared AI use that falls within the scope permitted by this assessment brief will not negatively affect your mark. However, using AI and not declaring this can have significant consequences for your mark and your degree. 

4. Arrangements for submission  

Deadline 

Your individualised submission deadline – which includes any extensions – will be shown in the Submission Status table at the bottom of this Assessment Brief.  

Submission Process 

Your assessment must be submitted online via Moodle. Please name the files you are submitting using the convention:

MODULE CODE_ASSESSMENT NUMBER_STUDENT NUMBER (plus _FILE NUMBER if more than one file is being submitted) 

Please ensure you do not use special characters (e.g. brackets, commas, speech marks, question marks, etc.) in the file name. 

The Maximum file size for uploading is 100 MB  

For help with submitting your assignment, please see How to Submit Assessments. 

Submission Statement 

When you submit a piece of work for assessment, you are confirming your acceptance of the Assessment Submission Declaration.  

Anonymity 

This assessment will be marked anonymously. This means Moodle will hide student identities from the marker during the marking process. To protect this anonymity, please do not incorporate your personal details, including your name, initials and email anywhere within the actual assignment or its file name. 

Please also be aware that if you take an extension, it may not be possible for your work to be marked anonymously. This is because the technology reveals all student identities to the marker on the standard feedback return date, but your assignment might be marked after this.   

If you have any questions about anonymous assessment, please contact your Module Tutor.  

Extensions 

This assignment is eligible for a self-certified, 7-day EC1 and/or DA1 extension; to self-certify, see EC1 and/or DA1. 

5. How your assignment will be marked 

This assessment will be adjudged on how well it meets the requirements in Section 2 and attends to the following assessment criteria, which reflect measures of excellence across a range of core skills for a Level 6 assignment:

Writing/Presentation Style 

80+ 

Extraordinary and authoritative understanding of the five selected software errors, including root causes, exploit mechanisms, secure mitigations, verification, the Security Development Life Cycle and product risk. 

Exceptional critical analysis of vulnerable and mitigated code. Exploitation, appropriate tool-assisted testing and retesting are interpreted rigorously; security decisions, limitations and residual risks are evaluated convincingly. 

Extensive, independent and critical research. Authoritative standards and high-quality academic or technical sources are integrated throughout to substantiate technical claims, mitigations, lifecycle discussion and risk analysis. 

Lively, precise and professional report with exceptional control of argument. Code, screenshots and tool results are clearly captioned, referenced and explained; appendices are complete; virtually error-free. 

70–79 

Excellent and wide-ranging understanding of the five software errors, their causes, exploitation, mitigation and retesting, with confident understanding of secure development lifecycle and risk principles. 

Excellent and consistent analysis of practical evidence. Vulnerable and mitigated code, exploits, security-tool findings and retests are interpreted critically, with well-justified controls and thoughtful consideration of limitations and residual risk. 

Excellent independent research using a wide range of relevant academic and authoritative technical sources. Evidence is selected critically, integrated effectively and used consistently to support claims. 

Excellent, coherent and professional presentation with strong control of argument. Figures, code and test evidence are consistently captioned, referenced and discussed; source-code appendices are complete; very few errors. 

60–69 

Very good understanding of the selected vulnerabilities, exploitation, mitigation and verification. Secure development lifecycle and risk concepts are accurately explained, with only minor gaps or inaccuracies. 

Very good analysis of code and testing evidence, mainly consistent throughout. Appropriate tools and retesting support the conclusions, although evaluation of alternatives, limitations or residual risks may lack some depth. 

Very good independent research using a good range of relevant academic and authoritative technical sources. Most technical claims are supported and sources are generally integrated effectively. 

Very good, logical and professional report with few language or formatting errors. Code, screenshots and results are generally well captioned, referenced and explained, with an appropriate appendix. 

50–59 

Sound understanding of the main vulnerabilities and their mitigations. Most exploitation and retesting processes are explained correctly, although some technical, lifecycle or risk concepts lack depth or precision. 

Sound analysis with some interpretation of vulnerable code, exploits, tool findings and before-and-after testing. Decisions are generally reasonable but justification, critical comparison or evaluation of tool limitations is uneven. 

Some independent research using an appropriate range of mostly relevant sources. Key concepts are supported, although source quality, breadth, currency or integration is inconsistent. 

Sound and generally coherent presentation with some language or formatting errors. Most practical evidence is identifiable and discussed, but captioning, cross-referencing, labelling or appendix organisation is inconsistent. 

40–49 

Basic but sufficient understanding of the five software errors. Core causes, attacks and mitigations are identified, but explanations contain omissions or inaccuracies and lifecycle, risk or verification knowledge is limited. 

Basic analysis and collation of practical evidence. Some testing and retesting are demonstrated, but interpretation is mainly descriptive, tool use is limited or uncritical, and mitigation claims are not always convincingly verified. 

Basic independent research using a limited range of relevant sources. Some claims are supported, but important technical assertions are uncited and source quality or referencing accuracy is variable. 

Broadly coherent report with noticeable language and presentation errors. Code and screenshots are included, but some are poorly captioned, insufficiently explained or disconnected from the narrative; appendices may be incomplete. 

30–39 

Limited and partly inaccurate understanding, with major omissions across the five errors, exploitation, mitigation or retesting. Secure development lifecycle and product-risk discussion is superficial or mechanically reproduced. 

Predominantly descriptive work with limited or unreliable testing. Tool outputs or screenshots receive little interpretation; mitigations and retesting are weak or incomplete, and important conclusions lack verifiable practical evidence. 

Limited research with over-reliance on generic websites, lecture material or a narrow source base. Strong assertions are frequently unsupported and sources are poorly selected, integrated or referenced. 

Limited structure and coherence. Vulnerable and mitigated versions are difficult to distinguish; code or screenshots are often uncaptioned or unreferenced; appendix, formatting or required referencing conventions are inadequately followed. 

20–29 

Very limited subject knowledge with many errors and omissions. Few assessment elements are meaningfully completed, and the relationships between vulnerability, exploitation, mitigation and verification are poorly understood. 

Very limited attempt to analyse the task. Practical demonstrations are incomplete, invalid or unsupported; little meaningful testing, tool interpretation, mitigation evaluation or retesting is evident. 

Little credible research. Discussion relies mainly on personal opinion, unreliable or inappropriate sources, with few supported technical claims and seriously deficient citation practice. 

Little structure or coherence. Practical evidence is difficult to follow, important code or results are missing or unexplained, and frequent language and formatting problems substantially hinder understanding. 

10–19 

Seriously flawed knowledge of secure coding. The submission shows little accurate understanding of the selected vulnerabilities or of how they are exploited, tested, mitigated and retested. 

Seriously flawed attempt at analysis that does not meaningfully address the task. There is no credible interpretation of code, testing evidence, security-tool findings, mitigations or risk. 

It is unclear whether meaningful reading has taken place. Credible sources are absent or not cited, and technical claims are largely unsupported or materially inaccurate. 

Much of the submission cannot be understood because of seriously flawed structure, language or presentation. Required practical evidence, source code or report components are largely absent or unusable. 

1–9 

No meaningful secure-coding knowledge or practical understanding is demonstrated. 

No meaningful attempt is made to analyse or address the practical and critical requirements of the assessment. 

No credible reading or research is apparent or cited. 

The submission is fragmentary, illegible or does not follow the required assessment format. 

0 

Assessment not submitted.
